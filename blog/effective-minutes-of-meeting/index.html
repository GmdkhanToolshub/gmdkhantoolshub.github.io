How to Write Minutes of Meeting That Drive Action

What to include in minutes of meeting, a simple template, and how to track decisions and action items so meetings lead to results.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 5 November 20264 min read

Minutes of meeting (MoM) are not a transcript. Their job is to record what was decided and who will do what, so that the meeting continues to have an effect after everyone leaves the room.

What every set of minutes should contain

  • Meeting title, date, time and location or link.
  • Attendees and apologies.
  • Agenda items or topics discussed, in brief.
  • Decisions made, stated clearly.
  • Action items, each with an owner and a due date.
  • Risks or issues raised, and the date of the next meeting.

A simple workflow

  1. Prepare. Create the template with the agenda before the meeting so you only fill in details.
  2. Capture as you go. Note decisions and actions as they are said. Mark them in your notes so they are easy to find.
  3. Confirm actions out loud. Before closing, read back each action, its owner and its date.
  4. Distribute quickly. Send within 24 hours while memories are fresh.
  5. Follow up. Review open actions at the start of the next meeting.

Write actions so they can be completed

Compare “Look into distributor claims” with “Share the reconciled claims list for the top five distributors with Finance by Friday”. The second names a deliverable, a recipient and a date. Vague actions are the main reason follow-up fails.

Keep it short

One or two pages is plenty. Readers should be able to find their own actions in under a minute. Bullets and a table for actions beat long paragraphs.

Tracking over time

Keep a running action log with status (open, done, overdue). Highlighting overdue items in the next meeting creates accountability without blame, because the data is simply on the table.

A template you can copy

This structure works for most recurring business meetings. The content below is an invented example.

SectionExample
MeetingMonthly commercial review, 5 March, 10:00, Meeting Room 2
AttendeesRegional managers, Finance lead, Supply chain lead
ApologiesMarketing lead
Key pointsNorth region is behind plan because of a stock shortage on two products
DecisionPrioritise available stock to North for the next two weeks
Next meeting2 April, 10:00

Then list the actions in a table of their own:

#ActionOwnerDueStatus
1Confirm revised dispatch plan for NorthSupply chain lead7 MarchOpen
2Share reconciled claims list for top five distributorsFinance lead8 MarchOpen

Separate discussion, decisions and actions

The most useful habit in minute-taking is keeping these three apart. A discussion point records what was considered. A decision records what was agreed. An action records who will do what by when. When they are blended into one paragraph, readers have to work out for themselves whether something was settled, and two people can walk away with different understandings of the same meeting.

What to leave out

  • Word-for-word debate. Summarise the conclusion and any key reason behind it.
  • Who said what, unless a person asked for their view to be recorded or the meeting's governance requires it.
  • Emotional language. Minutes should describe issues neutrally, even when the conversation was heated.
  • Your own opinion. If you need to add context, mark it clearly as a note from the minute-taker.

Handling sensitive topics

Leadership meetings can cover pay, restructuring, disputes or commercial terms. Agree with the chair who should receive the minutes, and consider a separate restricted note for sensitive items rather than circulating them widely. If your organisation has legal or compliance requirements for records, follow those first.

Common mistakes

  • Assigning an action to a team rather than one named person.
  • Using "as soon as possible" instead of a date.
  • Sending minutes days later, after people have already acted on their own memory of the meeting.
  • Starting the next meeting without reviewing open actions.
  • Writing everything in the past tense, so that open actions look like completed work.

Example email to send with the minutes

Hello all, please find the minutes of today's commercial review attached. There are two actions, listed at the top with owners and due dates. Please reply by tomorrow evening if anything needs correcting. We will review open actions at the start of our next meeting on 2 April.

Short, specific and easy to answer: it tells readers what is attached, what they need to do and when the follow-up happens.

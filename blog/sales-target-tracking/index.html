Sales Target Tracking: Using the Daily Run Rate Method

Learn the daily run rate method for tracking sales targets: achievement, remaining target, required daily sales and projections, with simple examples.

By Ghulam Mohiuddin NiaziPublished 5 November 20264 min read

Monthly targets are easy to set and easy to lose track of. By the middle of the month it is clear whether you are ahead or behind, but not always clear how far behind or what to do about it. The daily run rate method gives you three numbers that answer those questions.

The three numbers

  1. Achievement % = achieved ÷ target × 100.
  2. Required daily sales = remaining target ÷ selling days left.
  3. Current run rate = achieved ÷ selling days completed.

A worked example

Suppose the monthly target is 5,000,000 with 26 selling days. After 15 days you have achieved 3,000,000.

MeasureCalculationResult
Achievement3,000,000 ÷ 5,000,00060%
Remaining5,000,000 − 3,000,0002,000,000
Days left26 − 1511
Required per day2,000,000 ÷ 11≈ 181,818
Current run rate3,000,000 ÷ 15200,000

Because the current run rate (200,000) is higher than the required rate (about 181,818), the month is on track if the pace holds.

Reading the gap

If the required rate is much higher than the current run rate, there are three choices: increase activity, change the mix, or reset expectations early. Spotting this at day fifteen leaves time to act; spotting it on day twenty-five does not.

Cautions

  • Sales are not always evenly spread. Month-end loading, promotions and seasonal patterns can make early run rates misleading.
  • Use selling days, not calendar days, and adjust for holidays.
  • Track by territory or product as well as in total; a healthy total can hide a weak area.

Adding a projected finish

The three numbers above tell you where you stand. A projection estimates where you will end up if nothing changes.

Projected finish = current run rate × total selling days

In the example, 200,000 × 26 = 5,200,000, or 104% of target. That single figure is often the easiest way to explain the month to senior leaders, provided you state the assumption behind it: the current pace continues.

Why the total can hide a problem

Now split the same example across two territories.

TerritoryTargetAchievedRequired per dayCurrent run rateStatus
Territory A2,000,0001,300,000≈ 63,636≈ 86,667Ahead
Territory B3,000,0001,700,000≈ 118,182≈ 113,333Slightly behind
Total5,000,0003,000,000≈ 181,818200,000On track

The total is on track, yet Territory B is not. If Territory A slows after reaching its own target, the overall result is at risk. Reviewing this table at day fifteen gives the manager time to act, and it can raise a useful question: can extra effort or stock go to where it is needed most?

Handling uneven sales patterns

A straight-line run rate assumes each selling day is equal, which is rarely true. If your business usually sells more in the final week, an early run rate will look worse than it really is. A simple improvement is to use history.

  1. Look at the last few comparable months.
  2. Calculate what share of the month's sales had typically been reached by this day.
  3. Divide current sales by that share to get a pattern-based projection.

For example, if 50% of monthly sales are normally reached by day fifteen, then 3,000,000 ÷ 0.5 suggests a finish of 6,000,000. Comparing this with the straight-line 5,200,000 shows a realistic range. Be careful, though: heavy month-end sales can themselves be a warning sign if they come from loading distributors rather than real demand.

Counting selling days correctly

  • Remove weekly off-days and public holidays for the market concerned.
  • Agree on whether today counts as completed, and apply this consistently.
  • Check whether all regions share the same calendar, since local holidays can differ.
  • Note unusual events, such as a system outage or supply shortage, so that the run rate is read in context.

Making the numbers lead to action

A tracker is only useful if it changes what people do. When a territory is behind, ask a few focused questions: is the gap in coverage, in order size, or in the product mix? Is stock available? Which customers can realistically order more this month? Record the agreed actions with an owner, then check the required rate again a few days later to see whether the gap has narrowed.

Frequency

A weekly view suits most teams early in the month. Moving to a daily view in the final week helps when each day matters, but daily reporting for the whole month can create noise and take time away from selling.

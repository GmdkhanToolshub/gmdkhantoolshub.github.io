Business Intelligence vs Business Analytics Explained

How business intelligence and business analytics differ and overlap, shown with one worked data set: what happened, why, what may happen and what to do.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 25 November 20266 min read

On this page7 min read

  • The short answer
  • Four questions
  • What happened?
  • Why did it happen?
  • What may happen?
  • What should we do?

A company says it needs "analytics". One person means a monthly dashboard, another a sales forecast, a third a recommendation on pricing. Without a shared meaning, the wrong thing gets bought or built.

The short answer

In everyday use, business intelligence (BI) usually refers to collecting, organising and presenting business data so people can see what is happening. Business analytics (BA) usually refers to using statistical and other methods to explain why, estimate what may happen and support choices about what to do.

The boundary is not fixed. IBM's explanation describes business analytics as a subset of business intelligence. Microsoft's introduction to BI says BI tools often include several kinds of analysis, including predictive. Other organisations draw the line differently. When it matters, ask what questions must be answered, not which label to use.

Four questions

QuestionOften calledTypical output
What happened?DescriptiveReports, dashboards, comparisons
Why did it happen?DiagnosticBreakdowns, drivers, investigation
What may happen?PredictiveForecasts and probabilities, with uncertainty
What should we do?PrescriptiveOptions, scenarios, recommendations

We can follow all four with one made-up data set: six months of figures from the example in the monthly reporting guide.

Download the data used in this guide (.xlsx)

1. What happened?

June revenue was 1,088,000 against a budget of 1,040,000, and 8.6% above May. Operating profit was 181,300, which was 12,700 below budget.

This is reporting, the core of most BI work. It needs clear definitions, complete data and a trusted calculation. It is not "basic" in the sense of unimportant: every later step rests on it. Here it already shows something worth asking about: revenue beat budget but profit missed.

2. Why did it happen?

Break the gap into parts. Gross profit was 7,300 above budget, but operating expenses were 20,000 above, which gives the 12,700 shortfall.

Within cost of goods, materials were 36,400 over budget. That figure has two different parts. The budget set materials at 55% of product sales. June product sales were 960,000 against a budget of 920,000.

Part of the materials varianceWorkingAmount
Selling more than planned40,000 extra sales × 55%22,000
Materials taking a larger share960,000 × (56.5% − 55%)14,400
Total36,400

The first part is expected when sales rise. The second needs investigation. The arithmetic still does not give the cause. A higher share could come from supplier prices, discounts that lowered selling prices, waste, a shift towards products that use more material, or an accounting timing difference. A breakdown shows where to look. Finding the cause needs evidence.

3. What may happen?

A very simple prediction fits a straight line through six months of revenue:

Month123456
Revenue930,000905,000988,0001,030,0001,002,0001,088,000

Excel's FORECAST.LINEAR function gives about 1,102,800 for month 7. The line rises by about 32,086 a month.

One quick test is to hide the latest month and forecast it from the others. Using months 1 to 5, the method would have predicted 1,051,700 for month 6. The actual figure was 1,088,000, so the forecast was 3.3% too low.

Do not read that as "this model is accurate to about 3%". It is a single test. Six points cannot reveal seasons, one-off orders, price changes or a change in the market, and a straight line will continue rising for ever. A responsible forecast states its assumptions, is tested on more history and gives a range, and it is compared with a simple alternative such as "same as last month".

4. What should we do?

Prescriptive work compares actions. Suppose the team asks what June would have looked like if materials had stayed at 55% of product sales, with everything else unchanged: cost would have been 14,400 lower, and operating profit 195,700 instead of 181,300, slightly above the budget of 194,000.

This is a what-if calculation, not a promise or a recommendation. It assumes the lower cost share was achievable without reducing sales or quality. A decision would need the verified cause, the options for fixing it, their costs and risks, and who is responsible. Some prescriptive methods use optimisation models; many business decisions use scenarios like this plus judgement.

How the work differs in practice

AreaBI emphasisBA emphasis
Main needA shared, current viewUnderstanding and choosing
Typical workData models, dashboards, definitions, accessInvestigation, statistics, forecasting, scenarios
RhythmRepeated reportingOften organised around a question
Main riskTrusted-looking figures that are wrong or unusedConfident conclusions the data cannot support

These are tendencies, not job boundaries. In a small business one person often does both, sometimes in the same spreadsheet.

Which should come first?

If people disagree about basic figures, fix definitions and data first. A forecast built on disputed data only moves the argument. If reporting is trusted but decisions are still guesses, spend effort on analysis. In practice they develop together: analysis often reveals a data problem, which improves the reporting.

  1. Name one decision that needs better information.
  2. List the figures it needs and agree their definitions.
  3. Check that the data exists, is complete enough and may be used for this purpose.
  4. Build the smallest report that answers "what happened".
  5. Investigate one important change.
  6. Add prediction only if a decision depends on the future, and test it.
  7. Record what was decided and check the result later.

Do you need special tools?

Not to start. A spreadsheet can handle small examples like this one. Dedicated BI tools such as Microsoft Power BI help with connecting sources, sharing and refreshing reports. Statistical or programming tools help with larger or more complex analysis. A tool does not supply good definitions, clean data or a sensible question.

Advantages and limits of each

BI gives consistent, repeatable visibility, but can become a collection of unused dashboards and usually will not explain a change by itself. Analytics can improve understanding and test options, but needs more skill and care, and can sound more certain than it is.

Common mistakes

  • Buying software before naming the decision.
  • Treating correlation or a breakdown as proof of cause.
  • Giving a forecast as a single number with no assumptions or test.
  • Assuming a more complex method is a better one.
  • Ignoring privacy and access when combining data.
  • Never checking whether a recommendation worked.

Frequently asked questions

Is business analytics part of business intelligence?

Some definitions, including IBM's explanation, say so. Others treat them as overlapping or separate. State what you mean in your own project.

Is a dashboard BI or analytics?

A dashboard that reports results is usually called BI. If it also provides breakdowns, forecasts or scenarios, it supports analytics too.

Can Excel be used for business analytics?

Yes, for many small tasks, including breakdowns, simple forecasts and what-if calculations. Size, repeatability, collaboration and method may require other tools.

Is predictive analytics the same as AI?

No. Prediction can use simple statistics, as here, or machine learning. The method should fit the question and be tested.

Which skills should I learn first?

Learn to define a measure, check data, summarise it and explain a comparison. Then learn basic statistics and how to question a forecast.

Why was the forecast not given with a range?

Six points are too few to estimate a trustworthy range here. The example shows the mechanics and a limitation; it should not be used to plan a business.

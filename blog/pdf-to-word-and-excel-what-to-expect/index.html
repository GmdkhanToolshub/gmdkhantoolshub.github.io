PDF to Word and Excel: What to Expect

How PDF conversion really works, why some files convert well and others do not, and a step-by-step method for getting editable Word and Excel files from a PDF.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 20 November 20265 min read

A PDF is designed to look the same everywhere. That is its strength and also why editing one is awkward. When someone sends you a report as a PDF and you need to change a paragraph, or a bank statement as a PDF and you want to total the amounts, you need to convert it into a Word or Excel file. Converters can do a good job, but only when you know what they are working with. This guide explains what happens during conversion, which files convert well, and how to check the result.

How a PDF stores information

Inside a PDF, a page is a set of instructions: draw this text at this position, draw this line, place this picture. There is no built-in idea of a paragraph, a heading or a table. A converter has to guess the structure by looking at where pieces of text sit on the page. It groups characters into lines, lines into paragraphs, and decides that larger text is probably a heading. For tables, it looks for neat vertical gaps between columns of text.

This explains why simple documents convert almost perfectly and complicated layouts do not. A plain letter has obvious paragraphs. A magazine page with three columns, text boxes and pictures does not.

The most important check: is it text or a picture?

Open the PDF and try to select a word with your mouse. If the words highlight, the PDF contains real text and conversion should work. If you cannot select anything, the pages are pictures, such as a scan or a photograph. A converter that only reads text cannot get words out of a picture. Recognising text in images needs optical character recognition, which is a different process. For scans, you may need to retype the content, or use a scanner app that recognises text before you convert.

Converting PDF to Word

  1. Open the free PDF to Word tool and add a text-based PDF. It is processed in your browser and not uploaded.
  2. Decide whether to detect headings. This is useful for reports and articles, less so for plain letters.
  3. Choose whether each PDF page should start a new Word page. Keep it on if you want the page breaks to match, and off if you plan to edit and reflow the text.
  4. Convert and download the .docx file, then open it in Word, Google Docs or LibreOffice.
  5. Read the result against the PDF. Look at headings, lists, tables, page numbers and special characters.

Expect to tidy a few things. Bullet lists may need re-applying, tables may need adjusting, and fonts may differ from the original because the converter cannot always reuse the document's fonts. This is normal and usually much faster than retyping.

Converting PDF to Excel

  1. Open the PDF to Excel tool and add a PDF that contains a table as real text.
  2. Choose one sheet for everything, or one sheet per page. For a long statement with a continuous table, one sheet is usually easier to total.
  3. Keep the option that turns numbers into real Excel numbers. Otherwise, figures stay as text, and sums and sorting will not work.
  4. Check the preview of the first rows, then download the .xlsx file.
  5. In Excel, test your numbers: add up a column and compare the total with the PDF.

A worked example

You receive a three-page supplier statement as a PDF. It lists date, invoice number, description and amount, with a total at the bottom. After converting to Excel, you check the preview and see that the amount column has been read as numbers. In the workbook, you add a SUM formula over the amount column. The result is 482,350, and the printed total says 482,350. They match, so you can trust the conversion and go on to filter by date or sort by amount. If they had not matched, you would look for a row where a number was read as text or a cell had been merged with its neighbour.

Things that commonly go wrong

  • Merged or shifted columns. If a description is long, the converter may put part of it in the next column. Fix these by hand.
  • Numbers with symbols. Currency signs and commas may keep a number as text. Use Excel's Text to Columns or Find and Replace to clean them.
  • Headers repeated on every page. Statements repeat column titles. Delete the extra header rows before you total.
  • Missing characters. Special symbols and some Urdu or Arabic script depend on fonts. Check these carefully in the result.
  • Complex layouts. Multi-column designs, text boxes and overlapping graphics rarely survive. Consider copying the key content manually.

Always compare against the original

Whatever tool you use, treat the converted file as a draft. Compare it with the PDF page by page for anything important: names, figures, dates and clauses. A conversion that looks right can still have swapped a digit or dropped a line. For financial or legal material, verify every number that matters.

Going the other way: Word to PDF

When you have finished editing, you will probably want a PDF again to share a fixed layout. The Word to PDF tool turns a .docx file into a PDF with headings, lists, tables and pictures. Check page breaks and line wraps in the PDF, since fonts may be replaced with standard ones.

Privacy and sensitive documents

Statements, contracts and ID copies deserve care. Converting in your browser means the file stays on your device. Even so, delete the converted copies when you no longer need them, and avoid emailing spreadsheets that contain account numbers unless it is necessary.

Quick guide

  • Check that the PDF has selectable text before you start.
  • Pick PDF to Word for documents and PDF to Excel for tables.
  • Keep numbers as real numbers.
  • Compare totals and key facts with the original.
  • Expect to tidy layout, not to retype everything.

Used this way, conversion saves a lot of time. Try the free PDF to Word and PDF to Excel tools on your next document.

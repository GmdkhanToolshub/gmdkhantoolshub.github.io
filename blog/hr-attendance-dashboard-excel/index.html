How to Build an HR Attendance Dashboard in Excel

Turn monthly attendance data into an Excel HR dashboard with correct department totals, trends, follow-up counts, data checks and care for privacy.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 25 November 20267 min read

A manager asks, "How was attendance last month?" and receives a sheet with a row for every employee. It contains the answer, but nobody can see it, and it shows far more about individuals than most readers need. An HR attendance dashboard should show the overall picture, where attention is needed and whether the figures can be trusted, while limiting what it reveals about any one person.

This guide builds one from monthly records for a made-up organisation of 38 people in four departments, January to March 2026. It assumes your daily attendance is already recorded. If not, start with the attendance tracking guide. For general dashboard layout and charts, see the Excel dashboard guide. This page concentrates on what is different about people data.

Download the example HR dashboard (.xlsx)

1. Agree who the dashboard is for

A leadership team usually needs totals and trends. A department manager may need their own team. HR may need individual records to give support or apply a policy. These are different reports with different access. Do not give everyone the most detailed one.

For each measure, write down the definition, the data source, who owns it and what someone might reasonably do when it changes.

2. Prepare one row per person per month

The Data sheet has 114 rows, which is 38 people for three months. Each row has the month, employee ID, department, working days, days present, approved leave days, unapproved days and late arrivals. It uses IDs, not names, because the dashboard never needs a name.

A Row check column tests that days present + approved leave + unapproved days = working days. The dashboard shows "Data check: OK" only when every row passes. We tested this by changing one value in a copy: the message changed to show one wrong row. This check cannot find a person who is missing altogether, so also compare the number of people with your employee list.

3. Calculate rates from totals

Attendance = SUMIFS(days present) ÷ SUMIFS(working days)
Excluding approved leave = SUMIFS(days present) ÷ (SUMIFS(working days) − SUMIFS(approved leave))

In March the organisation's attendance was 96.7%. The simple average of the four department rates would be 97.2%, because it gives the four-person Accounts team the same weight as the fourteen-person Operations team. Always divide the totals.

4. Choose the headline tiles

The example shows six for the chosen month: people (38), attendance (96.7%), attendance excluding approved leave (99.0%), approved leave days (18), unapproved days (8) and late arrivals (18). The people count assumes one row per person per month, so a duplicate row would inflate it.

5. Look beyond the headline

This is where the sample becomes useful:

MonthAttendanceExcluding approved leaveApproved leave daysUnapproved daysLate arrivals
January97.5%99.6%18324
February96.8%99.5%20418
March96.7%99.0%18818

From February to March the main attendance figure barely moved, from 96.8% to 96.7%. In the same month, unapproved days doubled from 4 to 8. Two fewer days of approved leave hid much of the change. A dashboard that showed only the headline rate would have missed it.

Months also have different numbers of working days: 22, 20 and 21 in this sample. That is why rates are better than raw counts for attendance. For counts such as unapproved days, remember that headcount and working days can affect them too. Three months are not enough to show a lasting trend.

6. Compare departments fairly

Warehouse1194.8%97.3%61

The Warehouse team accounts for 6 of the 8 unapproved days, and one person there had two or more. The last column matters: the same total can come from many people with one day each or from one person with several, and those situations need different conversations. It is a reason to ask questions, not a finding of misconduct. Shift patterns, transport problems, illness recorded under the wrong code or a data error could all be behind it.

7. Handle small groups with care

In a very small team, a department figure can point to one person. The example hides departments with fewer than five people:

=IF($C10<Calc!$G$3, "Hidden", SUMIFS(…)/SUMIFS(…))

Five is a setting chosen for this example, in cell Calc!G3. It is not a legal standard.

Hiding a row is not enough on its own. Here the organisation had 8 unapproved days and the three visible departments account for 1 + 1 + 6 = 8. A reader can work out that Accounts had none. The workbook also contains every row on its Data sheet. To share results with a wider audience, send only the summary, check whether hidden figures can be worked out by subtraction, and consider combining small groups or leaving out the total that gives them away.

8. Add charts that answer a question

The example has two: attendance by month, with and without approved leave, and unapproved days by month. The percentage chart starts at zero, so small differences look small. If you need to show finer movement, use a table, or a clearly labelled line chart, rather than a shortened column axis. Check that each chart's title says what it counts. These two charts always show all three months, whatever month is selected above.

9. Decide what happens next

A dashboard is useful only if there is a fair, agreed response. For example:

  1. Check that the data is complete and correctly coded.
  2. Look at whether the change comes from many people or a few.
  3. Ask the relevant manager about work conditions in that period.
  4. Where an individual needs a conversation, have it privately, hear their side and follow your policy.
  5. Record the action and review it later.

Do not use a colour or a threshold to trigger discipline automatically.

10. Maintain it safely

  • Add each new month as new rows, then extend the Calc table, charts and month list.
  • For a new department, add a row to the department table and check that the people and unapproved totals still reconcile.
  • Add a year column before the data covers more than twelve months.
  • Store the file where access is limited and logged if possible.
  • Remove data you no longer need according to your retention rules.

Advantages and limits

Advantages: one consistent set of definitions, a quick monthly view and formulas that can be inspected.

Limits: manual updates, weak access control compared with an HR system, no automatic audit history and privacy risks when files are copied. The dashboard shows patterns, not causes, and cannot judge whether a recorded reason is correct. If many people need access, or each should see only their own team, use a system that can enforce permissions.

Common mistakes

  • Putting employee names on a leadership dashboard.
  • Reporting one attendance rate with no definition.
  • Averaging department percentages.
  • Ranking departments with very different shift patterns or headcounts without context.
  • Assuming a hidden row keeps its figures private.
  • Showing sick leave details for small groups.

Frequently asked questions

Which attendance rate should be the headline?

Use the one your policy defines, and show the other beside it. In the sample, they differ by more than two percentage points in March.

Should the dashboard rank employees?

This design does not. A ranking invites comparisons without context. Where individual follow-up is needed, handle it separately with limited access.

What minimum group size should I use?

There is no figure that suits every case. Consider how identifiable people are, what the figures could reveal and the rules that apply to you. Ask whoever is responsible for data protection.

Can I add turnover or overtime?

Yes, with their own definitions and checks. Add measures only when someone will use them, and avoid combining unrelated figures into one score.

Why does my total differ from the department table?

A department may be spelled differently, be missing from the table or be hidden by the small-group rule. Hidden departments are still included in the headline totals.

Can I use it with Excel for the web?

The workbook uses common functions, but it was checked only in the desktop version of Microsoft Excel (version 16.0). Test it where you plan to use it.

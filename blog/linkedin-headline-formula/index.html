How to Write a Strong LinkedIn Headline

Your LinkedIn headline appears wherever your name does. Learn a simple formula, see before-and-after examples and avoid mistakes that make profiles forgettable.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 20 November 20264 min read

On LinkedIn, your headline is the line of text that appears under your name in search results, comments, connection requests and messages. It is often the only thing a recruiter reads before deciding whether to click your profile. By default, LinkedIn fills it with your current job title, such as "Executive at ABC Company". That is accurate but forgettable. A little thought turns it into a short advertisement for what you do and who you help.

What the headline does

A good headline answers three silent questions from the reader: who are you, what are you good at, and why should I care? It also helps search. LinkedIn matches the words in your headline when recruiters search for skills or job titles, so including the right keywords makes you easier to find. You have a limit of 220 characters, but the first part is what shows in many places, so put the most important words first.

A simple formula

Use this pattern as a starting point:

Role | Specialty or industry | Key skills | Result or who you help

You do not need every part. Pick the ones that are true and useful. The vertical bar or a small separator keeps the line easy to scan.

Before and after examples

  • Before: Manager at XYZ Distribution. After: Sales Operations Manager | FMCG distribution | Excel and Power BI | Turning sales data into clear targets.
  • Before: Student at a university. After: Final-year Business student | Marketing and data analysis | Looking for an internship in Lahore.
  • Before: Accountant. After: Accountant | Tax filing and payroll for small businesses | Helping owners stay compliant.
  • Before: Looking for opportunities. After: HR Officer | Recruitment and onboarding | 3 years in retail and telecom.

Notice that each "after" says what the person does, names a field or skill, and gives the reader something specific to remember.

Step by step

  1. Write your target. Decide what you want to be found for. Is it a job title, such as Data Analyst, or a type of work, such as inventory planning?
  2. List three to five keywords. Look at job adverts for roles you want and note the repeated skills and tools. Use the ones that are genuinely yours.
  3. Choose your anchor. This is your role or a clear identity, for example "Project Manager".
  4. Add proof or audience. A short result or the group you help makes you memorable.
  5. Trim. Remove filler such as passionate, hardworking and dynamic. Keep nouns and tools.
  6. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a person talking about their work, it is good.

A worked example

Take Sana, who works in customer support at a telecom company and wants to move into customer success. Her headline reads "Customer Service Representative at TelecomCo". She lists her skills: complaint resolution, CRM software, Excel reporting and Urdu and English communication. She decides her target is customer success roles in technology or services. Her new headline: "Customer Support Specialist | Telecom | CRM and Excel reporting | Turning complaints into loyal customers". It keeps her real job, includes searchable skills and signals the direction she wants to go. She can change it again once she moves into the new role.

Keywords without keyword stuffing

Search matters, but a headline crammed with unrelated terms looks desperate. Include only the skills and titles you can back up in your profile and interviews. A headline with five relevant keywords reads better than one with fifteen. Match the language that employers use: "Data Analyst" is more searchable than "Numbers Wizard".

Be careful with these choices

  • "Seeking opportunities". It says little and signals that you have no focus. If you are job hunting, state the role you want instead.
  • Buzzwords. Words like guru, ninja and visionary do not help a recruiter understand you.
  • Overstating. A false title or claim can be discovered quickly and harm your reputation.
  • Too many roles. If you list five unrelated jobs, no one knows who you are. Choose a main identity.
  • Typos. The headline is short, so one mistake is very visible.

Match the rest of your profile

The headline is the doorway, and the About section and experience are the room. Make sure the skills and results you mention in the headline appear again in your summary and in your job descriptions. Add a clear profile photo and a tidy About section. If you are also updating your CV, the Resume Builder helps you keep both documents consistent, and our guide on ATS-friendly resumes explains how keywords work in applications.

When to update it

Review your headline when you change jobs, gain a new skill or certification, or begin a job search. A quick check every few months is enough. After editing, view your profile as a visitor would, and search for yourself using the keywords you included to see where you appear.

Checklist

  • Role or identity first.
  • Two to four honest keywords.
  • A result, specialty or audience.
  • No filler words.
  • Reads well in under 15 seconds.
  • Consistent with your CV and About section.

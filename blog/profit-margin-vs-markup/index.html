Profit Margin vs Markup: What Small Businesses Get Wrong

The difference between profit margin and markup, the formulas for each, a worked pricing example and how to avoid underpricing your products.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 10 November 2026Updated 25 November 20265 min read

On this page5 min read

  • A worked example
  • The common mistake
  • Quick conversion

Many small businesses mix up margin and markup, and the mistake quietly reduces profit. Both use the same cost and price, but they answer different questions and give different percentages.

The two formulas

  • Markup % = (selling price − cost) ÷ cost × 100. It tells you how much you add on top of the cost.
  • Margin % = (selling price − cost) ÷ selling price × 100. It tells you how much of each sale is profit.

A worked example

A product costs 800 and you sell it for 1,000.

MeasureCalculationResult
Profit1,000 − 800200
Markup200 ÷ 80025%
Margin200 ÷ 1,00020%

Same sale, two different percentages. A 25% markup is only a 20% margin.

The common mistake

Suppose you want a 30% margin and add 30% to your cost. A product costing 800 is then priced at 1,040, which is a margin of only about 23%. To get a true 30% margin, divide the cost by 0.70: 800 ÷ 0.70 ≈ 1,143.

Quick conversion

MarginMarkup needed
20%25%
30%about 43%
40%about 67%
50%100%

Do not forget the other costs

Margin on the product is not your final profit. Rent, salaries, delivery, payment fees, discounts and returns come out of it. Work out your gross margin first, then check what is left after those costs.

Converting between the two

Write both as decimals, so 25% is 0.25.

  • Markup from margin: margin ÷ (1 − margin). A 20% margin is 0.20 ÷ 0.80 = 0.25, a 25% markup.
  • Margin from markup: markup ÷ (1 + markup). A 50% markup is 0.50 ÷ 1.50, a margin of about 33.3%.

Margin can never reach 100% while the product costs you something. Markup has no upper limit.

In Excel or Google Sheets

Put the cost in B2 and the selling price in C2, then format the two result cells as percentages.

You wantFormula
Markup=(C2-B2)/B2
Margin=(C2-B2)/C2
Price for the margin in D2=B2/(1-D2)

If a price or cost can be blank, wrap the formula in IFERROR, but check the blank cells instead of hiding them for good.

Pricing when there are fees and delivery

Some costs are a fixed amount per order, such as delivery. Others are a share of the selling price, such as a payment or marketplace fee. They go in different places in the formula:

Price = (product cost + fixed costs per sale) ÷ (1 − target margin − percentage fees)

Example: the product costs 800, delivery costs you 100, the fee is 5% of the price and you want 30% left. The price is 900 ÷ (1 − 0.30 − 0.05) = 900 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 1,385. Check it: the fee is about 69, and 1,385 − 900 − 69 leaves about 416, which is 30% of 1,385. Real fee rates differ between providers and may carry tax, so use the figures from your own agreement.

What a discount really costs

Go back to the product that costs 800 and sells for 1,000. A 10% discount drops the price to 900, so profit falls from 200 to 100. The discount is 10%, but you lose half of the profit on each unit. You would need to sell twice as many to earn the same total.

The units you need, compared with before, are: old margin ÷ (old margin − discount), with the discount measured against the old price.

DiscountAt a 20% marginAt a 40% margin
5%1.33 times the units1.14 times the units
10%2 times the units1.33 times the units
15%4 times the units1.6 times the units

If the discount equals the margin, there is no product profit left at any volume. The table also leaves out extra costs that higher volume can bring, such as more staff time or delivery.

Do not average margins

Suppose product A has sales of 100,000 at a 40% margin, and product B has sales of 300,000 at 10%. The simple average of the two margins is 25%, but it is wrong. Profit is 40,000 + 30,000 = 70,000 on sales of 400,000, an overall margin of 17.5%. Always divide total profit by total sales. When your sales mix moves towards lower-margin items, the overall margin falls even if no price has changed.

Frequently asked questions

Is margin or markup always the bigger number?

For a sale above cost, markup is always bigger, because it divides the same profit by the cost, which is smaller than the price. If you sell below cost, both are negative.

Should the cost include tax?

Treat cost and price the same way. Many businesses compare both without sales tax they collect or can claim back. Tax rules depend on your registration, so confirm yours with a tax adviser.

Is gross margin the same as net margin?

No. Gross margin takes away the cost of the goods sold. Net margin takes away all other expenses too, so it is usually much lower.

What counts as a good margin?

There is no single figure. It depends on your trade, your running costs and how fast stock sells. A useful test is whether your total gross profit covers your running costs and leaves enough over.

Can I use the same margin on every product?

You can, but many sellers do not. A fast-selling item may work on a thinner margin, while a slow or fragile one may need more. Watch the overall margin of the whole mix.

Which one should you use?

Use markup when you set prices from costs. Use margin when you report performance and compare products, because it is directly linked to sales. Whichever you choose, say which one you mean when you talk to partners or staff.

How to Merge and Split PDF Files

A practical guide to combining several PDFs into one, pulling out the pages you need, and avoiding the mistakes that waste time, with a free browser tool.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 20 November 20265 min read

Most people meet the same two PDF problems sooner or later. A job portal wants one file, but you have a CV, a cover letter and three certificates saved separately. Or you receive a 60-page report and only need pages 12 to 18 to forward to a colleague. Merging and splitting solve both, and neither needs expensive software. This guide explains how each one works, what to check before you start, and how to avoid the small mistakes that cost time.

When merging makes sense

Merging means joining several PDF files, in an order you choose, into a single document. It is the right move when a recipient expects one attachment, when pages belong together as a package, or when you want to keep a record in one place. Common examples include:

  • A job application pack: cover letter first, then CV, then degrees and experience letters.
  • A fee or visa submission with a form, a bank slip and a copy of an identity document.
  • A monthly expense claim made of many separate receipts that were scanned one by one.
  • A client proposal where the quotation, terms and company profile were written in different programs.

Merging is not the right tool if you only need to reduce file size. Joining files does not shrink them; the result is roughly the sum of the parts. If size is the problem, compress afterwards with the PDF Compressor.

Step by step: merging PDFs

  1. Open the free PDF Merge and Split tool and choose Merge.
  2. Drop all the files in, or click to choose them. Everything is processed inside your own browser, so the documents are not uploaded to a server.
  3. Put the files in the order you want. This is the step people rush. Read the list from top to bottom as the recipient would read the pages.
  4. Create the merged file and download it.
  5. Open the result and scroll through every page. Check the page count against what you expected, for example 2 + 1 + 3 pages should give 6.

When splitting makes sense

Splitting is the reverse. You start with one large PDF and produce smaller ones. There are three typical reasons:

  • Extracting a section. Send only the pages that matter, such as the summary of a report or one chapter of a book you are allowed to share.
  • Meeting upload limits. Many portals cap attachments. Two files of 4 MB each can succeed where one file of 8 MB fails.
  • Separating combined scans. A scanner may produce one file for a whole stack of documents. Splitting returns each document to its own file.

When you split, you normally describe the pages you want. Typing 2-4 extracts pages two to four. A list such as 1,3,5 picks single pages. Splitting every page produces one file per page, which the tool can give you as a ZIP.

Step by step: extracting pages

  1. Choose Split in the tool and add your PDF.
  2. Type the pages you want, or choose to split into single pages or parts.
  3. Download the new PDF, or the ZIP if several files were made.
  4. Open one of the results and confirm that the first and last pages are the ones you meant.

A worked example

Imagine an application for a job abroad. You have a 2-page CV, a 1-page cover letter, a 4-page degree transcript scanned sideways, and a 3-page set of reference letters. The employer wants a single PDF under 5 MB.

First, fix the sideways transcript with the PDF Rotator, so that nothing is upside down in the final file. Next, merge in the order cover letter, CV, transcript, references. That gives 10 pages. Check the count. If the combined file is, say, 9 MB, run it through the compressor on a medium setting and compare the size again. Finally, open the result and zoom in on small print such as grades or dates to be sure the text is still sharp.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Wrong order. Fixing order after merging means starting again. Arrange first.
  • Password-protected files. A protected PDF usually has to be unlocked first with the password you were given. If you do not have the right to remove the protection, do not try to.
  • Merging private documents into a shared file. Before you send a combined file, scroll through every page. It is easy to leave an unrelated bank statement at the end.
  • Forgetting the file name. Name the result clearly, for example Ali-Khan-Application-2026.pdf, not merged(3).pdf.
  • Assuming the file is smaller. It is not. Compress separately if needed.

Does merging reduce quality?

A well-built merge copies the pages as they are. Text stays selectable, images keep their resolution and links keep working, because nothing is redrawn. Quality only falls when you also compress, and even then you control the level. This is why the order of operations matters: merge first, check, and compress last if you must.

Privacy: why browser tools help

Documents such as identity cards, contracts and bank statements are sensitive. A tool that runs in your browser reads the files on your own device and builds the new PDF there. Nothing has to travel to a server. That does not remove every risk, since your device and the file you send still matter, but it avoids handing copies to an unknown website.

Quick checklist

  • Put the pages in the right order before you merge.
  • Rotate any sideways pages first.
  • Check the page count after merging or splitting.
  • Zoom in to confirm the text is readable.
  • Compress last, only if you need a smaller file.
  • Give the final file a clear name.

With these habits, combining and extracting pages takes a couple of minutes and the result looks professional. Try it now with the free PDF Merge and Split tool.

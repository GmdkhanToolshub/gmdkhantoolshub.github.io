How to Create a Strong Password

What makes a password strong, why length beats complexity, how password managers and two-step login help, and how to build passwords that are safe and usable.

On this page5 min read

  • Three kinds of good password
  • Use a password manager
  • Turn on two-step login

Passwords protect your email, bank, social media and work accounts, yet most people still choose them the same way: a pet's name, a birth year, a small variation of an old password. Attackers know this and make use of it. The good news is that a strong password is not difficult to create once you understand what makes one strong. This guide explains the ideas in plain language and gives you a practical setup you can follow today.

How passwords get broken

There are three common ways. First, guessing: automated programs try millions of likely passwords, starting with common ones such as 123456 and password and with patterns like a name followed by a number. Second, reuse: when a website is breached and your password leaks, attackers try the same email and password on other sites. Third, tricking you: fake login pages and messages that ask you to type your password. A strong password defends mainly against the first, and good habits defend against the other two.

Length matters more than tricks

Strength is often described in bits of entropy, which measures how many guesses an attacker would need. Each extra character in a random password multiplies the number of possibilities. A random password of 16 characters drawn from lowercase letters, uppercase letters, digits and symbols has roughly 100 bits of entropy, which is far beyond practical guessing. A short password with clever substitutions, such as P@ssw0rd!, is weak because attackers already try those substitutions.

The key word is random. Humans are bad at randomness. A password built from a computer's secure random generator has no pattern to exploit.

Three kinds of good password

  1. Random strings from a generator. The Password Generator uses your browser's cryptographic random generator. Choose 16 or more characters and let a password manager remember it.
  2. Passphrases. Four or more unrelated words, such as "violet kettle harbour seven", are long, easy to type and easy to remember. Use random words, not a quote or a song line.
  3. A master password. If you use a password manager, you need one strong passphrase to protect it. Make this one long and unique and never reuse it.

Use a password manager

You cannot remember dozens of long, random passwords, and you should not try. A password manager stores them in an encrypted vault, fills them in for you and warns you about reuse. Browsers include basic managers, and there are dedicated ones that work across devices. Pick one from a reputable provider, protect it with a strong master passphrase, and keep a recovery method somewhere safe, such as a printed code stored with your important papers.

Turn on two-step login

Two-step verification asks for a second proof besides your password, usually a code from an authenticator app or a security key. Even if someone learns your password, they cannot get in without it. Turn it on first for your email, because email is how you reset every other account, then for banking, social media and work systems. Authenticator apps are generally safer than codes sent by text message, which can be intercepted in some situations.

A worked example

Take the case of an online shop account. Open the generator, set the length to 16, tick all four character types and generate. You might get something like a random mix of letters, digits and symbols. Copy it straight into your password manager when you create the account, rather than typing it from memory. For your email, use a four-word passphrase you can type. Then switch on two-step login with an authenticator app. In ten minutes you have a unique password for each account, no reuse, and a second layer of defence. If one shop is breached, only that one password is exposed, and it is useless anywhere else.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Reusing passwords. This is the single biggest risk. One breach should never unlock everything.
  • Personal information. Names, birthdays, phone numbers and your city are easy for others to guess or look up.
  • Predictable changes. Changing Summer2026 to Autumn2026 does not make a password new.
  • Storing passwords in plain notes or messages. Someone with access to your phone or chat history could read them.
  • Typing passwords on links from messages. Go to the site yourself instead.
  • Sharing codes. No genuine bank or service will ask you to read out a one-time code.

What to do if you think a password was exposed

  1. Change it right away on that site, and on any other site where you used the same one.
  2. Turn on two-step login if it was not already on.
  3. Check your account's recent activity and sign out of unknown devices.
  4. Look at your email forwarding and recovery settings in case someone changed them.
  5. Be alert for messages that look like they come from the affected service.

Privacy of the tool itself

A password generator is only useful if it does not record what it creates. Ours runs in your browser and does not send the password anywhere. Even so, treat any generated password as secret: copy it into your manager and do not leave it in a document.

Your five-minute plan

  • Install or enable a password manager.
  • Create a long, unique master passphrase.
  • Replace the passwords on your email, bank and social accounts with generated ones.
  • Turn on two-step login for those accounts.
  • Stop reusing passwords from now on.

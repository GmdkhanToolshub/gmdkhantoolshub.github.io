JPG vs PNG vs WebP: Which Format to Use?

The real differences between JPG, PNG and WebP, when each one is the best choice, and how to convert between them without wasting quality or file size.

By Gmd Khan Niazi (Ghulam Mohiuddin Khan Niazi)Published 20 November 2026Updated 25 November 20264 min read

Every photo, screenshot and logo you save is stored in some format, and the choice affects file size, sharpness and where the image can be used. Most people never choose on purpose; they keep whatever the camera or screenshot tool produced. That is fine until a form rejects your file, a web page loads slowly, or a logo looks fuzzy. This guide compares the three formats you will meet most often: JPG, PNG and WebP.

JPG in plain words

JPG (also written JPEG) is built for photographs. It reduces file size by throwing away detail that the human eye is unlikely to notice, such as tiny colour variations in a sky. You choose how much to throw away with a quality setting. At a high setting the picture looks almost identical and the file is much smaller than the original. At a very low setting you start to see blocky patches and a smudgy look around edges.

Strengths: small files for photos, accepted almost everywhere, from government portals to old email programs. Weaknesses: no transparency, and every time you edit and re-save a JPG the quality can fall slightly. Text and sharp lines can look slightly rough when the quality is low.

PNG in plain words

PNG keeps every pixel exactly as it was. Nothing is thrown away, so the quality is perfect, but photographs become large files. PNG also supports transparency, which is why logos and icons with no background are usually PNG.

Strengths: crisp text and lines, perfect for screenshots, diagrams, logos and anything with flat colours; transparency support. Weaknesses: large files for photos, which can be several times the size of the same picture saved as JPG.

WebP in plain words

WebP is a newer format designed for the web. It can behave like JPG, losing a little detail to save space, or like PNG, keeping everything and supporting transparency. In many cases a WebP file is noticeably smaller than a JPG or PNG that looks the same, which is why websites like it: smaller files load faster, especially on mobile data.

Strengths: usually the smallest file, supports transparency and quality control. Weaknesses: some older software, print shops and upload forms do not accept it, so you may need to convert it back.

Side-by-side comparison

  • Photos: JPG or WebP. PNG is wasteful.
  • Screenshots with text: PNG, or WebP at high quality.
  • Logos and icons with transparent backgrounds: PNG or WebP.
  • Images for forms, CNIC-style uploads, job portals: JPG, because it is accepted nearly everywhere.
  • Website images where speed matters: WebP, with JPG as a fallback if needed.
  • Anything you will edit many times: keep an original in PNG, then export JPG or WebP copies.

  2. Drop in one image or a whole batch. The pictures are decoded in your browser and are not uploaded.
  3. Choose the target format: JPG, PNG or WebP.
  4. For JPG and WebP, set the quality. Around 75 to 85 percent is a good starting range for photos.
  5. If you convert a transparent PNG to JPG, choose the background colour that will fill the transparent areas, usually white.
  6. Convert and download. Open the result and compare it with the original.

A worked example

You take a screenshot of a spreadsheet. The file is a PNG of about 1.8 MB, and an email system limits attachments. Because the content is text and lines, converting to JPG at a low quality would make the writing fuzzy. Converting to WebP at around 80 percent quality is likely to keep the text readable while making the file much smaller. If the recipient cannot open WebP, you can convert the same picture to JPG at a higher quality setting and accept a somewhat larger file.

Now take a 5 MB phone photo for an online application. Here JPG at about 75 percent, perhaps combined with a smaller width, will bring the file well under a 1 MB limit while still looking clean. The Image Compressor and Image Resizer are built for this job.

Common mistakes

  • Saving photos as PNG. The files balloon with no visible benefit.
  • Re-saving a JPG again and again. Keep your original and export copies from it.
  • Converting a low-quality image to a better format. Changing JPG to PNG does not bring back lost detail; it only makes the file bigger.
  • Forgetting about transparency. A logo saved as JPG gets a solid background, often white, which looks wrong on a coloured page.
  • Using the wrong size. A 4000-pixel-wide photo on a small website slows the page down. Resize first.

Which one should you remember?

If you only keep one rule, use this: JPG for photographs you need to share widely, PNG for graphics and screenshots you need to keep sharp, and WebP when you control where the image will be used and want the smallest file. Keep an original, convert copies, and always open the converted file before you send it.
